Ground improvement in Albuquerque is necessitated by the region’s complex subsurface conditions, which include deep alluvial deposits from the Rio Grande rift, collapsible soils, and expansive clay layers. These soils often exhibit low bearing capacity, high compressibility, and susceptibility to moisture-induced volume changes. The presence of a shallow water table further complicates construction, particularly for infrastructure such as roadways, bridges, and commercial developments. Technical context is shaped by the city’s seismic hazard zone, where liquefaction potential in loose saturated sands demands mitigation. Effective ground improvement cannot merely densify soils but must also address environmental constraints, including protection of the Rio Grande riparian corridor and prevention of groundwater contamination. A thorough site-specific geotechnical investigation is mandatory to characterize soil stratigraphy, groundwater conditions, and collapsibility potential, informing the selection of appropriate improvement techniques tailored to Albuquerque’s unique geotechnical regimen.

Common ground improvement methods in Albuquerque range from vibratory techniques to chemical stabilization. Deep dynamic compaction is frequently employed to densify granular fills and collapsible soils, utilizing heavy tampers dropped from cranes to reduce void ratios. For fine-grained or layered deposits, stone columns with or without geotextile encasement are installed to improve shear strength and provide drainage, mitigating liquefaction risk. Compaction grouting, involving low-slump grout injected under pressure to displace and densify surrounding soil, addresses localized settlement issues. In areas with high groundwater, deep soil mixing using lime or cement binders creates stabilized columns or panels. Jet grouting is also applied to form in situ soilcrete elements for underpinning and cutoff walls. Each method must be validated through field trials and performance monitoring, with emphasis on achieving uniformity and meeting design strength parameters.

Local standards governing ground improvement in Albuquerque are defined by the New Mexico Building Code and the City of Albuquerque’s Grading, Drainage, and Erosion Control Ordinance. Geotechnical design must adhere to IBC 2021 requirements for site class, liquefaction hazard, and collapsible soil mitigation. Additionally, the Albuquerque Geotechnical Design Guidelines mandate that all ground improvement projects verify compliance through penetration test or pressuremeter tests, with minimum relative density or unconfined compressive strength thresholds. Environmental regulations restrict disposal of soil and groundwater during improvement, necessitating containment and treatment plans. The New Mexico Environment Department oversees contaminant migration risks, particularly when grouting materials are used near the aquifer. These standards ensure that ground improvement systems are robust, adaptable to local geological hazards, and integrated with sustainable construction practices.

Applications of ground improvement in Albuquerque span critical infrastructure and urban development. For the Albuquerque Rapid Transit (ART) corridor, compaction grouting and deep soil mixing stabilized the highway subgrade under overhead bridges and at station foundations. In the urban infill of the Downtown and Sawmill districts, stone columns mitigated liquefaction prior to constructing mid-rise buildings. Along the Rio Grande levee system, dynamic compaction and wick drains reduced settlement and increased slope stability during flood risk reduction projects. At the Albuquerque International Sunport, deep soil mixing created load transfer platforms for new taxiways and runway expansions. In residential subdivisions on the West Mesa, controlled modulus columns were used to support structures on collapsible soils. Each application demonstrates that ground improvement is integral to Albuquerque’s resilience, enabling safe and economical construction on otherwise challenging subsurfaces.
