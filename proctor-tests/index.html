Last year we took over a fill job near the Petroglyph National Monument where the contractor was struggling with settlement cracks after every monsoon rain. The problem wasn't the soil type—the sandy silts there compact fine—it was that nobody had established a proper moisture-density relationship before placing lift after lift. We pulled samples and ran a Standard Proctor in the lab. The numbers told the whole story: the crew had been compacting 4 percent dry of optimum. No amount of extra passes was going to fix that. In Albuquerque, with our semi-arid climate, 10 inches of annual rainfall, and fill materials that vary from blow sand to clayey gravel, guessing at compaction is a fast track to rework. The Proctor test gives you the maximum dry density and optimum moisture content that your spec has to hit. Whether it's Standard (ASTM D698) for landscape pads and utility trenches or Modified (ASTM D1557) for structural fill under footings, we run the curve that matches your project’s compaction requirement. Once that curve is in hand, our field techs can verify every lift with a sand cone density test and you know within minutes if you are in spec or not.

If you are compacting without a current Proctor curve for the exact material you are placing, you are not doing quality control—you are just moving dirt around.

Methodology and scope

ASTM D698 and D1557 define the compaction energy, mold size, and rammer weight that separate Standard from Modified Proctor. For most commercial building pads in Albuquerque, the geotechnical report will specify a minimum of 95 percent of Standard Proctor maximum dry density, referencing ASTM D698 Method A or C depending on the percent oversize material. What trips up a lot of earthwork subs here is the gradation of the native fill. The Rio Grande basin deposits we excavate can shift from a well-graded sand to a gap-graded silty sand within 50 feet horizontally. If the material changes mid-job, the original Proctor curve is no longer valid. We see it all the time on projects along the I-25 corridor. You have to re-sample and re-run the test or your density readings become meaningless. The lab procedure itself is straightforward: we dry the material, mix it at incrementally higher moisture contents, compact each point in a 4- or 6-inch mold with a 5.5-lb or 10-lb rammer, and plot the wet density against moisture to find the peak of the curve. The result is a single pair of numbers—maximum dry density in pounds per cubic foot and optimum moisture as a percentage—that drives every compaction acceptance decision on site. For deep excavations where fill will support a heavily loaded mat foundation, we often recommend the Modified Proctor to account for the higher confining pressures at depth.

Local considerations

The most expensive mistake we see on Albuquerque earthwork jobs is a contractor ordering a single Proctor test at the start of a six-month project and then running the entire job off that one curve. The borrow source changes. The cut exposes a lens of finer material. The stockpile gets contaminated with surface organics. Suddenly the lab curve no longer matches what the compactor is rolling. The field density tests start failing. The contractor blames the technician, the technician blames the soil, and the owner gets a 30-day delay while everyone argues. Here is the hard truth: ASTM D698 and D1557 require a new Proctor any time the soil classification changes by more than a few percentage points of fines content. In the layered alluvial deposits of the Albuquerque basin, that can happen every few thousand cubic yards. We recommend running a check Proctor at least once a week on big fills and immediately when the color, texture, or plasticity of the material changes. The cost of a re-run is trivial compared to re-compacting 10,000 yards of fill that failed acceptance.

Applicable standards

The applicable ASTM standards are D698-12(2021) for standard effort compaction and D1557-12(2021) for modified effort compaction.

Associated technical services

01

Standard Proctor (ASTM D698)

For landscape fills, utility trench backfill, and lightly loaded slabs. We determine the moisture-density relationship using 12,400 ft-lbf/ft³ compaction effort. Includes full particle-size analysis on the same material so you have a defensible reference curve for field density acceptance.

02

Modified Proctor (ASTM D1557)

For structural fill under footings, retaining wall backfill, and pavement subgrade where higher density is required. We apply 56,000 ft-lbf/ft³ of compaction energy. The higher effort pushes optimum moisture down and maximum density up—critical for projects with tight settlement tolerances.

How often do I need a new Proctor curve during a fill operation?

Any time the soil classification changes visibly—color, grain size, plasticity—you need a new curve. In Albuquerque’s alluvial soils, we recommend a minimum of one check Proctor per 5,000 cubic yards of placed fill, or immediately if the material source shifts from one cut area to another.

Can I use the same Proctor curve for sand and clay on the same site?

No. A curve developed for a clayey sand will not be valid for a clean sand or a silty clay. The optimum moisture and maximum dry density shift significantly with fines content. You need a separate Proctor for each distinct material type you are placing as engineered fill.

What is the difference between Standard and Modified Proctor?

The difference is compaction effort. Standard Proctor (ASTM D698) uses a 5.5-pound hammer dropped 12 inches onto three layers of soil. Modified Proctor (ASTM D1557) uses a 10-pound hammer dropped 18 inches onto five layers. The Modified effort compacts the soil more densely, producing a higher maximum dry density at a lower optimum moisture content.
